Over 5 Percent of New Yorkers Have Lost SNAP Benefits

Nearly 100,000 New Yorkers no longer receive SNAP benefits as of January 2025. Many are getting reduced benefits.

By Niya Doyle

May 20th, 2026

Joel from the Bronx has been receiving SNAP benefits ever since a serious accident left him disabled in 2021. When his monthly allowance was cut from $292 to $150 with no explanation in August 2025, it began a long, arduous back-and-forth appealing closed cases and starting new ones with New York’s Human Resource Administration (HRA).

“I was told, ‘maybe it was a mistake you were getting the $292,’ but my disability income is well below poverty, so I’d always received close to the full SNAP benefit for years,” said Joel, who asked to keep his last name private.

After asking AI, he found out that he had a right to a Fair Hearing from HRA, which is a chance for an individual to tell a judge from the New York State Office of Temporary and Disability Assistance, Office of Administrative Hearings, why they think a decision about a case made by a local social services agency is wrong. After multiple rescheduling attempts, his hearing is now scheduled for May 26th.

The number of New Yorkers enrolled in the Supplemental Nutrition Assistance Program, also known as SNAP, has declined by over 5 percent since January 2025. This comes on the heels of the Big Beautiful Bill that was passed July 4th of last year, which included massive cuts to federal social programs such as Medicaid and SNAP and stricter eligibility requirements. The Wall Street Journal reported that enrollment in SNAP has decreased by almost 3.5 million since the bill was enacted. In New York City, nearly 90,000 New Yorkers no longer receive SNAP benefits.

While SNAP caseload fluctuations aren’t uncommon in New York City, according to New York City’s HRA, the acute drop in enrollment is directly related to provisions within the Big Beautiful Bill. No longer do certain categories of lawfully present immigrants, refugees, and asylum seekers receive benefits. The bill also introduces a work requirement of 80 hours a month for able-bodied adults, which has been challenging to meet for SNAP recipients, most of whom are already working.

Homecrest Community Services, a non-profit organization that connects primarily Asian-American adults, youths, and families in southern Brooklyn to social services, reports that their clients have already been confused over the new work requirement policies. One elderly client in his 60s has struggled to document consistent work hours in a way the system accepts.

“Despite actively working, he has had difficulty meeting reporting standards and now risks losing his benefits,” said Lisa Zhen, Director of Social Services at Homecrest Community Center, describing his situation. These requirements often do not align well with the realities of irregular or part-time employment.”

Despite the struggles, HRA claims that no New Yorker has lost benefits due to the new federal work requirements.

But perhaps the biggest challenge coming to New York City and New York state is the shift of administrative costs to fund SNAP. Typically, the federal government bears the burden of funding SNAP, splitting the costs 50/50 between states. Starting next year in 2027, the federal government will reduce its share to 25 percent, further straining New York State’s already rigid budget.

“I think New York is better than Arkansas in terms of being able to absorb those administrative costs, but they're significant,” said Dr. Nevin Cohen, Associate Professor, CUNY Graduate School of Public Health and Director of the CUNY Urban Food Policy Institute. “In the short term, it might be feasible, but in over the longer term, it's going to create hardship for the state.”

Alternatives to SNAP, such as food banks, are already struggling to meet demand. Food insecurity has risen in all five boroughs since 2023 due to a combination of inflation and a worsening affordability crisis. Food security, as defined by the New York City Department of Health, means access by all household members at all times to enough food for an active, healthy life. This also includes the “ready availability of nutritionally adequate and safe foods and the ability to consistently and reliably acquire suitable foods without having to rely on emergency food supplies, or other coping strategies.”

Data shows that between 2023 and 2025, the New York food-insecure population increased by an average of 16 percent, with the Bronx being the most food-insecure borough.

James Poisal, Director of Development and Communications of St. John’s Bread & Life, a community non-profit in BedStuy, Brooklyn, said that ever since the COVID-19 pandemic, the demand for food has only risen, noting that he sees “lots of fresh faces and working families.” 65% of the people who came to St. John’s Bread & Life in need of food or social assistance were working in some capacity.

Food prices are only expected to rise with the Strait of Hormuz closed—a vital passageway that ships 20 percent of the world’s oil and one-third of the world's fertilizer.

“It’s certainly bad everywhere, but New York doesn’t rely as much on a lot of the fertilizer right now,” said Poisal. “There’s not as much as a spike in cost…but in the Midwest, the cost of food has rise(sic) so much.”

For people like Joel, affording groceries and the cost of living together will only get harder unless there’s mass adoption of policies like city-owned grocery stores put forth by Mayor Mamdani or an overall effort from the federal government to address inflation.

“ “As someone living with disabilities, I am certainly grateful help exists…with grocery inflation, the real purchasing power of SNAP even at [my] full amount is only half what it was 20 years ago,” said Joel.
